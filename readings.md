# CSE 210B Winter 2027 — Candidate Reading List

This is a **superset** of 49 candidate papers, organized by topic and
following the arc of the course (see [`syllabus.md`](syllabus.md)). The
presented set (~18 papers, one per student-led session) will be down-selected
by the instructor before the quarter begins. Each entry gives the citation, a
link, a course-fit rating, an impact estimate, and a one-paragraph summary.
*See also* notes point at closely related work that could substitute for the
main entry.

**Course fit** (how well the paper serves *this* course, not paper quality):

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ★★★★★ | Core — should anchor a session; directly advances the course thesis |
| ★★★★☆ | Strong pick — excellent seminar material, clear fit |
| ★★★☆☆ | Good optional — background, breadth, or substitutable coverage |
| ★★☆☆☆ / ★☆☆☆☆ | Not used: papers below the 3★ bar were cut before making this list |

**Impact** figures are approximate citation counts read from Semantic
Scholar / publisher index snapshots in July 2026. Labels are honest about
data quality: `~N` is a current-ish snapshot, `≥N` a stale lower bound,
*not retrievable* means no reliable number surfaced (often because the
index splits arXiv and published versions), and *too recent* marks
2025–26 papers where counts are meaningless. Interpret with the usual
caveats: counts favor older papers and ML-venue visibility, so a 2025
verification paper with 30 citations may matter more to this course than a
2021 benchmark paper with thousands.

The through-line of the list: LLMs make code cheap; specifications and
machine-checked proofs are how we make cheap code trustworthy — and LLMs are
now making the proofs cheap too.

## 1. Foundations: LLMs for code generation

How code-generating models work, how they're evaluated, and why the
benchmarks deserve skepticism.

### 1. Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on Code
*Mark Chen, Jerry Tworek, Heewoo Jun, et al. arXiv, 2021.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ≥1,000 citations (2022 snapshot; vastly higher today — foundational)

The paper that started the modern era: Codex, a GPT model fine-tuned on
public GitHub code (the engine behind the original GitHub Copilot), and
HumanEval, a benchmark of 164 hand-written programming problems graded by
functional correctness rather than text similarity. Codex solves 28.8% of
problems with one sample and 70.2% when allowed 100 samples — an early
signal that *sampling plus filtering* is a powerful paradigm, foreshadowing
the generate-and-verify loops that dominate this course. Also notable for a
candid limitations-and-hazards section (alignment, security, docstring-length
sensitivity) that reads presciently five years on.

### 2. Competition-Level Code Generation with AlphaCode
*Yujia Li, David Choi, Junyoung Chung, et al. Science 378(6624), 2022.*
[DOI](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abq1158) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07814)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** ≥540 citations (2023 snapshot; higher today)

DeepMind's system for competitive programming: generate *millions* of
candidate programs per problem, then filter on the public test cases and
cluster by behavioral equivalence to pick at most ten submissions. Achieved a
median ranking in the top 54.3% of human participants in simulated Codeforces
contests. The interesting move for this course is architectural: brute
generation is useless without a cheap, mechanical correctness filter — here,
tests; later in the quarter, verifiers. AlphaCode is the ancestor of every
"sample until the checker accepts" pipeline we will read.

### 3. Is Your Code Generated by ChatGPT Really Correct? Rigorous Evaluation of Large Language Models for Code Generation
*Jiawei Liu, Chunqiu Steven Xia, Yuyao Wang, Lingming Zhang. NeurIPS 2023.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01210)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ~1,313 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

EvalPlus augments HumanEval with ~80x more test inputs via LLM-seeded,
type-aware mutation, producing HumanEval+. The added tests expose large
amounts of previously "passing" wrong code — pass@k drops by up to ~19–29%
across models — and even reveal incorrect ground-truth solutions in the
original benchmark. A crisp, discussable lesson in measurement validity: if
the field's headline numbers move this much when you merely add tests, what
would they do under formal specifications? Sets up the course's argument that
verification, not more tests, is the endgame for trusting generated code.

### 4. Large Language Models for Software Engineering: A Systematic Literature Review
*Xinyi Hou, Yanjie Zhao, Yue Liu, et al. ACM TOSEM 33(8), 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10620)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** ~746 citations (ACM DL, Jul 2026)

The standard reference survey of the LLM4SE explosion: 395 papers
(2017–2024) categorized by model, data pipeline, tuning/evaluation strategy,
and software engineering task. Useful less for any single result than as a
map — it shows how quickly the field concentrated on code generation and
repair while verification-adjacent tasks remained a small tail (a gap this
course lives in). Best assigned early and skimmed strategically; a good
candidate for the instructor-led week-1 background reading rather than a
student presentation.

## 2. Human factors and productivity

What actually happens when developers use these tools — including evidence
that cuts against the hype.

### 5. Grounded Copilot: How Programmers Interact with Code-Generating Models
*Shraddha Barke, Michael B. James, Nadia Polikarpova. OOPSLA 2023 (Distinguished Paper).*
[DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3586030)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** count not retrievable (widely cited)

A grounded-theory study observing 20 programmers using Copilot across tasks
in four languages. Its central finding has become the field's shared
vocabulary: interaction is bimodal, split between *acceleration* (the
programmer knows the plan and uses the AI to type it faster) and
*exploration* (the programmer is unsure and uses the AI to survey possible
approaches). Worth discussing which mode verified-programming assistants
should target — proof engineering arguably needs both, at different moments.

### 6. Expectation vs. Experience: Evaluating the Usability of Code Generation Tools Powered by Large Language Models
*Priyan Vaithilingam, Tianyi Zhang, Elena L. Glassman. CHI 2022 (LBW).*
[DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3491101.3519665)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** ~581 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

An early within-subjects study (n=24) with a result that still stings:
Copilot did not improve task completion time or success rate, yet most
participants preferred it anyway, valuing the starting points it provided and
the web searches it saved. The main obstacles were understanding, editing,
and debugging generated code. A compact seminar paper for discussing the gap
between perceived and measured productivity — a gap that reappears, larger,
in the METR study below.

### 7. Measuring GitHub Copilot's Impact on Productivity
*Albert Ziegler, Eirini Kalliamvakou, X. Alice Li, et al. CACM 67(3), 2024.*
[DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3633453)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** ~75 citations (Semantic Scholar; CACM version only, arXiv preprint indexed separately)

GitHub's own large-scale study, matching survey responses from 2,047 Copilot
users to fine-grained usage telemetry. The headline: suggestion acceptance
rate predicts perceived productivity better than any persistence-of-code
measure. Read critically — it is vendor research measuring *perceived*
productivity — but it defines the telemetry-plus-survey methodology the
industry now uses. *See also:* Peng et al.,
"[The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590)"
(arXiv 2023), the controlled experiment behind the widely quoted "55.8%
faster" claim.

### 8. Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity
*Joel Becker, Nate Rush, Beth Barnes, David Rein. METR report / arXiv, 2025.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)
**Course fit:** ★★★★★ · **Impact:** ~33 citations (Semantic Scholar; published Jul 2025)

The most important skeptical datapoint in the list: a randomized controlled
trial with 16 experienced maintainers completing 246 real tasks in mature
repositories they knew deeply. Allowing AI (mainly Cursor with Claude 3.5/3.7
Sonnet) made them **19% slower** — while they forecast a 24% speedup and,
even afterward, believed AI had sped them up ~20%. Essential calibration for
a course premised on 10–100x verification speedups: where do those gains
survive contact with expertise and mature codebases, and where do they
evaporate? Pairs perfectly with #7 in a single week.

## 3. Coding agents and large-scale software engineering

From single-file completion to agents that navigate, edit, and test whole
repositories.

### 9. SWE-bench: Can Language Models Resolve Real-World GitHub Issues?
*Carlos E. Jimenez, John Yang, Alexander Wettig, et al. ICLR 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770)
**Course fit:** ★★★★★ · **Impact:** ~1,183 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

The benchmark that reframed the field: 2,294 real GitHub issue/pull-request
pairs from 12 popular Python repositories, requiring multi-file,
repository-level edits validated by the projects' own tests. At publication
the best system resolved 1.96% of issues; frontier agents now resolve the
majority — a capability curve worth examining skeptically in class
(contamination, test weakness, Python monoculture). *See also:* OpenAI's
[SWE-bench Verified](https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/)
(2024), the human-validated 500-issue subset that became the de facto
leaderboard, and [SWE-Lancer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12115) (2025), which
prices tasks in real freelance dollars.

### 10. SWE-agent: Agent-Computer Interfaces Enable Automated Software Engineering
*John Yang, Carlos E. Jimenez, Alexander Wettig, et al. NeurIPS 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15793)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ~623 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

Demonstrates that the *interface* matters as much as the model: a
purpose-built agent-computer interface — structured file viewing/editing,
repository navigation, test execution — lifted GPT-4 to a then-state-of-the-art
12.5% on SWE-bench. The design lessons (guardrails, concise feedback, undo)
transfer directly to the verification setting: what is the right
"agent-verifier interface" for a model driving Dafny, Verus, or a proof
assistant? Good pairing with #12 for an agents-vs-pipelines debate.

### 11. AutoCodeRover: Autonomous Program Improvement
*Yuntong Zhang, Haifeng Ruan, Zhiyu Fan, Abhik Roychoudhury. ISSTA 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05427)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** ~146 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

Adds program-structure awareness to issue resolution: instead of treating
the repository as text, AutoCodeRover searches over ASTs (classes, methods)
and optionally uses spectrum-based fault localization to find where to edit,
then generates patches. Solved 19% of SWE-bench Lite at ~$0.43 per issue,
beating contemporaneous agents at a fraction of the cost. The seminar
question it raises: how much classical program analysis should we rebuild
into LLM pipelines rather than hoping the model infers it from tokens?

### 12. Agentless: Demystifying LLM-Based Software Engineering Agents
*Chunqiu Steven Xia, Yinlin Deng, Soren Dunn, Lingming Zhang. FSE 2025 (Distinguished Paper).*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01489)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** count not reliably retrievable (index entry fragmented)

The contrarian result: a deliberately *non*-agentic three-phase pipeline —
hierarchical fault localization, patch sampling, patch validation — that
outperformed most autonomous agents on SWE-bench Lite (32.0%) at about $0.70
per issue, and was adopted by OpenAI and DeepSeek for reporting their own
models' coding results. A sharp discussion paper on when agency (planning,
tool loops, self-direction) earns its complexity, and when a fixed pipeline
with good sampling is simply better engineering.

### 13. Coeditor: Leveraging Repo-level Diffs for Code Auto-editing
*Jiayi Wei, Greg Durrett, Isil Dillig. ICLR 2024 (Spotlight).*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18584)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** count not retrievable

From Isil Dillig's group: most real programming is *editing*, not writing
from scratch. Coeditor fine-tunes a code LM to predict edits to a region
conditioned on recent repository-level diffs, using a line-diff encoding and
static-analysis-derived context, trained on the commit histories of 1,650
Python projects. It lifts single-round edit-prediction exact-match from 34.7
(GPT-3.5) to 60.4. Conceptually important for verified software maintenance:
proof repair after a code change is exactly a "predict the edit from the
diff" problem.

## 4. LLM-powered testing, fuzzing, debugging, and repair

Dynamic correctness tooling — the pragmatic middle ground between "run it
and pray" and full verification.

### 14. An Empirical Evaluation of Using Large Language Models for Automated Unit Test Generation
*Max Schäfer, Sarah Nadi, Aryaz Eghbali, Frank Tip. IEEE TSE 50(1), 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06527)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** ~353 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

TestPilot generates JavaScript unit tests by prompting with a function's
signature, body, and docs, then *adaptively re-prompting* with failure
messages from the tests that didn't run — an early, clean demonstration of
the feedback loop that recurs throughout this course. On 25 npm packages it
reaches median 70.2% statement coverage versus 51.3% for the
feedback-directed baseline. Also a model methodology paper: careful
ablations, non-flaky measurement, and honest accounting of test quality
beyond raw coverage.

### 15. CoverUp: Effective High Coverage Test Generation for Python
*Juan Altmayer Pizzorno, Emery D. Berger. FSE 2025.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16218)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ~30–40 citations (Semantic Scholar; split across arXiv/FSE versions)

From Emery Berger's group: test generation driven by *coverage analysis in
the loop* — CoverUp interleaves per-iteration coverage measurement with LLM
dialogs that point the model specifically at still-uncovered lines and
branches. Achieves median per-module line coverage of 81% versus 62% for
CodaMosa, the prior hybrid search/LLM system. The general pattern —
cheap program analysis steering an expensive generative model — is one
students should carry into the verification section. *See also:* CodaMosa
(Lemieux et al., [ICSE 2023](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icse-2023/icse-2023-technical-track/132/CodaMOSA-Escaping-Coverage-Plateaus-in-Test-Generation-with-Pre-trained-Large-Langua)),
which seeds search-based test generation with LLM examples when coverage
plateaus.

### 16. Large Language Models Are Zero-Shot Fuzzers: Fuzzing Deep-Learning Libraries via Large Language Models
*Yinlin Deng, Chunqiu Steven Xia, Haoran Peng, et al. ISSTA 2023.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.14834)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** count not retrievable (heavily cited)

TitanFuzz's insight is elegant: an LLM trained on millions of programs is
implicitly a *generator of well-formed inputs* for any system whose inputs
are programs. Using Codex to synthesize seeds and an infilling model inside
an evolutionary loop, it fuzzes PyTorch and TensorFlow, improving API
coverage by 24%/91% over prior fuzzers and finding 65 bugs (44 confirmed,
previously unknown). Opens the "LLMs as test-input distributions"
conversation that Fuzz4All generalizes.

### 17. Fuzz4All: Universal Fuzzing with Large Language Models
*Chunqiu Steven Xia, Matteo Paltenghi, Jia Le Tian, et al. ICSE 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04748)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ~227 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

Generalizes TitanFuzz into the first *universal* fuzzer: LLM autoprompting
plus an LLM-powered mutation loop targeting nine systems across six input
languages — C, C++, Go, Java, Python, and, notably for us, SMT2. It found 98
bugs (64 confirmed previously unknown) in GCC, Clang, Z3, CVC5, OpenJDK, and
Qiskit. The Z3/CVC5 results deserve special attention in this course: our
entire verified-software story bottoms out in SMT solvers, and this paper
shows those trust anchors themselves harbor bugs.

### 18. ChatDBG: Augmenting Debugging with Large Language Models
*Kyla H. Levin, Nicolas van Kempen, Emery D. Berger, Stephen N. Freund. FSE 2025 (Distinguished Artifact).*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16354)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** count not retrievable

Berger's group puts an LLM *inside* the debugger: ChatDBG extends LLDB, GDB,
and Pdb so the model can take the wheel — issuing debugger commands to walk
stacks, inspect state, and test hypotheses — to answer open-ended questions
like "why is x null?". A single query yields an actionable fix 67% of the
time on Python benchmarks (85% with one follow-up). With 75,000+ downloads,
it is also a case study in shipping research tooling. Seminar angle: the
debugger is an *oracle-rich* environment; what is the equivalent
instrumentation for a proof assistant?

### 19. Automated Program Repair via Conversation: Fixing 162 out of 337 Bugs for $0.42 Each Using ChatGPT
*Chunqiu Steven Xia, Lingming Zhang. ISSTA 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00385)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ~231 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

ChatRepair reframes program repair as a conversation: feed the failing test,
its error output, and previously rejected patches back into the model,
asking either for a fix or — once a plausible patch passes — for variations
that guard against overfitting. State-of-the-art results on Defects4J at a
now-famous price point of $0.42 per bug. The economics slide of this paper
is the course thesis in miniature: when the marginal fix costs pennies, the
binding constraint becomes *validating* fixes — tests here, proofs later.
*See also:* "Teaching Large Language Models to Self-Debug" (Chen et al.,
[ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05128)) for repair guided by
self-explanation rather than external feedback.

## 5. Security and trust of AI-generated code

Why "it compiles and the tests pass" is not enough — the motivation for
everything after this section.

### 20. Asleep at the Keyboard? Assessing the Security of GitHub Copilot's Code Contributions
*Hammond Pearce, Baleegh Ahmad, Benjamin Tan, et al. IEEE S&P 2022.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.09293)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ~265 citations (Semantic Scholar; split across arXiv/S&P/CACM entries — true total higher)

The first systematic security audit of Copilot: 89 scenarios derived from
MITRE's top-25 CWEs, 1,689 generated programs, roughly **40% vulnerable**.
Beyond the headline number, the methodology (scenario design, CWE-grounded
scoring) became the template for a whole subfield. Five years later the
percentages have improved but the structural problem hasn't: models
reproduce the distribution of their training data, and that distribution
includes decades of vulnerable idioms. Discussion: which of these CWE classes
would a verifier have caught, and at what specification cost?

### 21. Do Users Write More Insecure Code with AI Assistants?
*Neil Perry, Megha Srivastava, Deepak Kumar, Dan Boneh. ACM CCS 2023.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03622)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ~222 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

The human side of the same problem: in a user study, participants with an AI
assistant wrote significantly less secure code than the control group — and
were *more confident* their code was secure. This automation-bias result is
arguably more dangerous than #20's: insecure suggestions are fixable;
miscalibrated trust compounds. *See also:* "Lost at C" (Sandoval et al.,
[USENIX Security 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09727)), whose
lower-level C study found assistants added at most ~10% more critical bugs —
a genuinely conflicting result worth reconciling in discussion.

### 22. We Have a Package for You! A Comprehensive Analysis of Package Hallucinations by Code Generating LLMs
*Joseph Spracklen, Raveen Wijewickrama, A H M Nazmus Sakib, et al. USENIX Security 2025.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10279)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** count not reliably retrievable (index entry fragmented)

A large-scale measurement of a failure mode unique to generative tooling:
across 16 models, 19.7% of packages recommended in generated Python and
JavaScript code *do not exist* — and the hallucinations repeat consistently,
enabling "slopsquatting" supply-chain attacks in which an adversary registers
the phantom package and waits. A vivid case study in how LLM failure modes
create qualitatively new attack surfaces, and a good prompt for discussing
which trust problems verification can and cannot address (a verified program
can still depend on a malicious library).

### 23. CRUST-Bench: A Comprehensive Benchmark for C-to-safe-Rust Transpilation
*Anirudh Khatry, Robert Zhang, Jia Pan, et al. (incl. Greg Durrett, Isil Dillig). COLM 2025 (Spotlight).*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15254)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** too recent for meaningful counts (2025)

From Dillig's group: 100 complete C repositories paired with hand-written
safe-Rust interfaces and test suites, so a successful transpilation must be
idiomatic, memory-safe, *and* functionally correct at repository scale.
Frontier models solve only ~13–22% of tasks single-shot (32–48% with repair
loops). Positioned here because safe-language migration is the
industrial-scale version of "make existing software trustworthy" — and the
gap between marketing and measured capability on this task frames the
course's central question about what verification-grade AI actually requires.

## 6. From natural language to specifications and invariants

If verification is becoming cheap, writing the *specification* becomes the
bottleneck — and the natural next target for LLMs.

### 24. Can Large Language Models Transform Natural Language Intent into Formal Method Postconditions?
*Madeline Endres, Sarah Fakhoury, Saikat Chakraborty, Shuvendu K. Lahiri. FSE 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01831)
**Course fit:** ★★★★★ · **Impact:** count not retrievable

Defines the *nl2postcond* problem — turning informal natural-language intent
into formal postconditions — along with metrics for both correctness and
*discriminative power* (does the postcondition actually reject buggy
implementations?). LLM-generated postconditions turn out to be generally
correct, able to discriminate buggy from correct code, and capable of
catching real historical bugs from Defects4J. A foundational reading for the
course's spec-inference arc, and a clean framework students can reuse in
their projects to evaluate their own AI-generated specifications.

### 25. SpecGen: Automated Generation of Formal Program Specifications via Large Language Models
*Lezhi Ma, Shangqing Liu, Yi Li, et al. ICSE 2025.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08807)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** count not retrievable

Generates verifiable JML specifications for Java programs through a
conversational phase — the LLM drafts specs, a verifier's feedback drives
revision — followed by mutation-based refinement of failed candidates.
Outperforms both purely neural and traditional (Houdini-style)
specification-inference baselines. Worth discussing the validation question
head-on: a spec the verifier accepts is consistent with the code, but is it
what the author *meant*? SpecGen sharpens the difference between verifiable
and correct specifications.

### 26. nl2spec: Interactively Translating Unstructured Natural Language to Temporal Logics with Large Language Models
*Matthias Cosler, Christopher Hahn, Daniel Mendoza, et al. CAV 2023.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04864)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** ~88 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

Tackles specification's hardest audience problem — temporal logic — with an
interactive twist: the LLM proposes LTL formalizations of natural-language
requirements and maps each subformula back to the natural-language fragment
it came from, so users repair the mapping rather than redrafting formulas
from scratch. A concrete design for keeping the human meaningfully in the
loop when neither party (human or model) can be fully trusted with the
formalization — a pattern that generalizes well beyond LTL.

### 27. Leveraging LLMs for Program Verification
*Adharsh Kamath, Nausheen Mohammed, Aditya Senthilnathan, et al. FMCAD 2024.*
[PDF](https://repositum.tuwien.at/handle/20.500.12708/200783) · [arXiv (earlier version)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07948)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** count not reliably retrievable (split arXiv/FMCAD entries)

The "Loopy" system: LLMs propose candidate loop invariants for C programs,
and sound symbolic tools do the gatekeeping — SMT-based checking, repair with
verifier feedback, and Houdini-style pruning of hallucinated conjuncts.
Verifies substantially more benchmarks than purely symbolic baselines. This
is the cleanest single illustration of the course's core architecture:
*unsound, creative generator + sound, mechanical checker = sound, practical
system*. *See also:* LaM4Inv (Wu et al.,
[ASE 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3691620.3695014)), which adds
bounded model checking to the filter loop and solves 309/316 benchmarks.

### 28. Ranking LLM-Generated Loop Invariants for Program Verification
*Saikat Chakraborty, Shuvendu K. Lahiri, Sarah Fakhoury, et al. Findings of EMNLP 2023.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.09342)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** ~42 citations (Semantic Scholar snapshot, possibly stale)

A systems-flavored complement to #27: if the model needs many samples to hit
a verifiable invariant, verifier calls dominate cost — so train a contrastive
ranker to sort candidates before checking any of them. Median rank of the
first verified invariant drops from 62 to 4 for GPT-3.5. A small paper with a
transferable lesson about the *economics* of generate-and-check loops: the
10–100x cost reduction this course is premised on comes not just from better
models but from engineering around expensive oracles.

### 29. Lemur: Integrating Large Language Models in Automated Program Verification
*Haoze Wu, Clark Barrett, Nina Narodytska. ICLR 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04870)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ≥13 citations (stale index snapshot)

Puts the LLM-plus-checker pattern on formal footing: a proof calculus whose
derivation rules say exactly when an LLM-proposed invariant or subgoal may be
admitted (only after validation by an automated reasoner), with a soundness
theorem for the combined system. For a PL audience this is the key
theoretical reading of the section — it shows the standard of rigor we should
demand before saying an AI-assisted verification pipeline is *sound*, rather
than merely usually right.

### 30. SatLM: Satisfiability-Aided Language Models Using Declarative Prompting
*Xi Ye, Qiaochu Chen, Isil Dillig, Greg Durrett. NeurIPS 2023.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09656)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** count not retrievable

From Dillig's group: rather than asking the LLM to *reason* (imperative
chain-of-thought, where each step can silently err), ask it to *formalize* —
emit a declarative logical specification of the problem — and hand the
solving to a SAT/SMT solver. This division of labor fixes the search and
planning errors that plague program-aided reasoning. Though framed as an NLP
paper, it is the purest statement of a thesis running through this whole
course: LLMs are better at translation into formal languages than at sound
inference, so architect systems accordingly. *See also (spec-first
programming):* Pythoness (Levin et al.,
[arXiv 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.02138)) and Flowco (Freund et al.,
[UIST 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14038)) from Emery Berger's group,
where developers author specifications and checked postconditions while the
LLM supplies the code.

## 7. Verified code generation: Dafny, Verus, and F*

The heart of the course: LLMs writing code *together with* the annotations
and proofs that make an auto-active verifier accept it.

**Background:** students unfamiliar with Rust-based verification should skim
"Verus: Verifying Rust Programs using Linear Ghost Types" (Lattuada et al.,
[OOPSLA 2023](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3586037)) before the Verus
papers below; Dafny background from CSE 210A plus its
[online tutorial](https://dafny.org/latest/OnlineTutorial/guide) suffices for
the Dafny papers.

### 31. Towards AI-Assisted Synthesis of Verified Dafny Methods
*Md Rakib Hossain Misu, Cristina V. Lopes, Iris Ma, James Noble. FSE 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00247)
**Course fit:** ★★★★★ · **Impact:** count not retrievable

The first careful study of LLMs writing *specified and verified* Dafny:
GPT-4 and PaLM-2 asked to produce method bodies, contracts, and the
annotations to verify them for 178 MBPP problems, under contextless,
signature-based, and retrieval-augmented chain-of-thought prompting. GPT-4
goes from 19% verified (no context) to 58% with CoT plus retrieved similar
examples, and the paper contributes the MBPP-DFY dataset. A natural first
paper for the section: it establishes both the feasibility and the prompt
sensitivity of verification-aware generation.

### 32. Clover: Closed-Loop Verifiable Code Generation
*Chuyue Sun, Ying Sheng, Oded Padon, Clark Barrett. SAIV 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17807)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ≥12 citations (ACM DL, published version only)

Proposes a *trust architecture* for generated code: check mutual consistency
among three artifacts — the code, its formal Dafny annotations, and its
natural-language docstring — using the verifier for code-vs-spec and LLM
equivalence checks for the natural-language legs. On CloverBench the checker
accepts 87% of correct examples and rejects 100% of incorrect ones. The
triangle is the interesting object: it attacks the "verified against the
wrong spec" problem (#25's worry) by making the informal intent a
first-class, checked participant.

### 33. dafny-annotator: AI-Assisted Verification of Dafny Programs
*Gabriel Poesia, Chloe Loughridge, Nada Amin. Dafny workshop @ POPL 2025.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15143)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** too recent for meaningful counts (2024–25)

Focuses on the grind that dominates real auto-active verification: inserting
the invariants and assertions that get an *existing* program through the
verifier. An LLM-guided greedy search adds annotations until Dafny accepts;
off-the-shelf LLaMa 3.1 8B succeeds on only 15.7% of DafnyBench methods, but
fine-tuning on DafnySynth — a synthetic dataset produced by open-ended LLM
program synthesis validated by Dafny itself — lifts this to 50.7%. The
data-flywheel argument (verifiers can *label their own training data* for
free) is a big idea worth a full discussion. *See also:* DafnyBench
(Loughridge et al., [TMLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08467)), the
750-program benchmark this work builds on.

### 34. AutoVerus: Automated Proof Generation for Rust Code
*Chenyuan Yang, Xuheng Li, Md Rakib Hossain Misu, et al. OOPSLA 2025.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13082)
**Course fit:** ★★★★★ · **Impact:** too recent for meaningful counts (2025)

A network of LLM agents that mimics how human Verus experts actually work —
preliminary proof generation, refinement with generic proof tips, then
error-guided debugging — to produce Rust/Verus proof annotations. On a new
benchmark of 150 non-trivial proof tasks it generates correct proofs for over
90%, most within 30 seconds. This is the strongest published evidence for the
course's 10–100x claim in the systems-verification setting. *See also:* the
earlier Microsoft study it grew from, Yao et al., "Leveraging LLMs for
Automated Proof Synthesis in Rust"
([arXiv 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03739)).

### 35. AlphaVerus: Bootstrapping Formally Verified Code Generation through Self-Improving Translation and Treefinement
*Pranjal Aggarwal, Bryan Parno, Sean Welleck. ICML 2025.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06176)
**Course fit:** ★★★★★ · **Impact:** too recent for meaningful counts (2025)

Attacks the data-scarcity problem head-on: bootstrap verified Verus
generation *without human annotations or fine-tuning* by translating from a
higher-resource verified language (Dafny), refining candidates via
tree search over verifier feedback ("Treefinement"), and — critically —
filtering out reward-hacked solutions that satisfy trivial or misaligned
specs. That last phase is a preview of a deep problem: when the checker is
the training signal, models learn to game the checker. Excellent late-quarter
discussion material on the limits of verifier-guided self-improvement.

### 36. Towards Neural Synthesis for SMT-Assisted Proof-Oriented Programming
*Saikat Chakraborty, Gabriel Ebner, Siddharth Bhat, et al. ICSE 2025 (Distinguished Paper).*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01787)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** count not retrievable

Scales the question to industrial proof-oriented code: a ~600K-line corpus
of open-source F* (including production code from Windows, Linux, Python,
and Firefox) yielding ~32K type-directed definition-synthesis problems.
Surprising result: fine-tuned *small* models (Phi-2, StarCoder) compare
favorably with GPT-4 at a fraction of the cost, especially with type-based
retrieval augmentation. Grounds a practical seminar debate — frontier models
via API versus cheap specialized models you can run in CI — that every
verification team is currently having.

### 37. Verified Code Transpilation with LLMs
*Sahil Bhatia, Jie Qiu, Niranjan Hasabnis, Sanjit Seshia, Alvin Cheung. NeurIPS 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03003)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ≥4 citations (stale index snapshot)

LLMLift revisits verified lifting: translate source programs into a DSL and
produce, alongside the translation, the invariants and equivalence assertions
a verifier needs to *prove* the translation correct — with Python as the
intermediate representation and no DSL-specific training. Solves 44/45
benchmarks across four DSLs, beating purpose-built symbolic tools while
running far faster. A tidy existence proof that "translation + proof of
equivalence" beats "translation + vibes," and a bridge between this section
and CRUST-Bench's repository-scale ambitions (#23).

### 38. Verina: Benchmarking Verifiable Code Generation
*Zhe Ye, Zhengxu Yan, Jingxuan He, et al. ICLR 2026.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23135)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** too recent for meaningful counts (2025–26)

The measurement paper for this whole section: 189 curated Lean tasks, each
with a problem description, reference implementation, formal specification,
and test suite, enabling *modular* evaluation of code generation, spec
generation, and proof generation separately and composed. The headline gap is
the course in one number: state-of-the-art models do well on code but prove
well under half the tasks. Assign late, when students can read the per-axis
results against their own project experience and judge whether the gap is
closing.

## 8. LLMs and interactive theorem proving

Coq/Rocq, Lean, and Isabelle — where CSE 210A meets the frontier. Students
already know the pain these systems automate away.

### 39. Baldur: Whole-Proof Generation and Repair with Large Language Models
*Emily First, Markus N. Rabe, Talia Ringer, Yuriy Brun. ESEC/FSE 2023 (Distinguished Paper).*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04910)
**Course fit:** ★★★★★ · **Impact:** ~61 citations (unattributed snapshot, low confidence)

A pivot point for the field: instead of predicting one tactic at a time
inside a search loop, fine-tune an LLM to emit *entire* Isabelle/HOL proofs
in one shot, plus a repair model that consumes the checker's error messages.
On 6,336 theorems, proves 8.7% more than the prior state of the art (Thor),
and — the number to discuss — reaches 65.7% of theorems in combination with
existing tools. Whole-proof generation with error-driven repair is now the
dominant paradigm; this is the paper that made the case. *See also:* Thor
(Jiang et al., [NeurIPS 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10893)), the
LM+hammer baseline Baldur improved on; and GPT-f (Polu & Sutskever,
[arXiv 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03393)) for the field's origin.

### 40. Draft, Sketch, and Prove: Guiding Formal Theorem Provers with Informal Proofs
*Albert Q. Jiang, Sean Welleck, Jin Peng Zhou, et al. ICLR 2023.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12283)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ~239 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

The paper that connected informal and formal proving: write (or generate) an
informal mathematical proof, map it to a formal *sketch* whose intermediate
claims structure the search, and let automated provers close the remaining
gaps. Then-state-of-the-art on miniF2F, with machine-drafted informal proofs
nearly matching human ones (38.9% vs 39.9%). The architecture — natural
language as scaffolding for formal search — is how practitioners actually
use LLMs with proof assistants today, and maps directly onto how students
should attack their course projects.

### 41. LeanDojo: Theorem Proving with Retrieval-Augmented Language Models
*Kaiyu Yang, Aidan Swope, Alex Gu, et al. NeurIPS 2023 (D&B).*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15626)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ~320 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

The infrastructure paper the Lean ecosystem runs on: open-source tooling for
programmatic interaction with Lean, a benchmark of 98,734 mathlib theorems
with fine-grained premise annotations, and ReProver — a retrieval-augmented
tactic generator trainable in about one GPU-week that beats non-retrieval
baselines and GPT-4. Premise selection (which of 100K library lemmas is
relevant *right now*?) is arguably the deepest retrieval problem in all of
AI, and this paper defines it cleanly. Also the natural on-ramp for any
student whose project targets Lean.

### 42. An In-Context Learning Agent for Formal Theorem-Proving
*Amitayush Thakur, George Tsoukalas, Yeming Wen, Jimmy Xin, Swarat Chaudhuri. COLM 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04353)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** count not retrievable

COPRA (from Swarat Chaudhuri's UT Austin group) asks how far a frontier
model can get *without fine-tuning*: GPT-4 drives a stateful backtracking
search, proposing tactics with execution feedback, search history, and
retrieved lemmas folded into each successive prompt — and it works in both
Lean and Coq. For our students the Coq support makes this immediately
hands-on. The agentic-search-vs-fine-tuned-generation contrast with #39/#41
sets up an excellent compare-and-contrast presentation pairing.

### 43. Proof Automation with Large Language Models
*Minghai Lu, Benjamin Delaware, Tianyi Zhang. ASE 2024.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14274)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ~21 citations (Semantic Scholar, Jul 2026)

PALM begins with a formative study of 520 failed GPT-3.5 Coq proofs and
extracts a usable diagnosis: models get the high-level proof structure
roughly right but fumble low-level details. The response is generate-then-
repair — LLM drafts the whole proof, targeted *symbolic* repair mechanisms
fix the details — evaluated on 10K+ theorems. The failure taxonomy alone is
worth the read: it tells students precisely where their CSE 210A intuitions
about proof structure remain load-bearing in the LLM era. *See also:*
"Getting More out of Large Language Models for Proofs" (Zhang, Ringer,
First, [AITP 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04369)), a short, readable
failure-mode study in the same spirit.

### 44. Rango: Adaptive Retrieval-Augmented Proving for Automated Software Verification
*Kyle Thompson, Nuno Saavedra, Pedro Carrott, et al. ICSE 2025 (Distinguished Paper).*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14063)
**Course fit:** ★★★★★ · **Impact:** too recent for meaningful counts (2025)

The strongest published Coq automation for *software* (not math)
verification: at every proof step, retrieve relevant premises and similar
proofs from the current project to condition a fine-tuned model, adapting
automatically to each codebase's idioms. Introduces CoqStoq (2,226 projects,
196,929 theorems) and proves 32% of test theorems — 29% more than Tactician.
For students who lived inside Coq in CSE 210A, this is the "how much of my
quarter could have been automated?" paper. *See also:* Graph2Tac
(Blaauwbroek et al., [ICML 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02949)) for the
online-learning alternative in the Tactician ecosystem, and CoqPilot
(Kozyrev et al., [ASE 2024 demo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19605)), a VS
Code plugin students can use in their projects today.

### 45. Automated Discovery of Tactic Libraries for Interactive Theorem Proving
*Yutong Xin, Jimmy Xin, Gabriel Poesia, et al. (incl. Isil Dillig). OOPSLA 2025.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.24036)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** too recent for meaningful counts (2025)

TacMiner (Dillig's group) attacks proof-engineering abstraction: mine a
corpus of Coq proofs for reusable custom tactics, using Tactic Dependence
Graphs that abstract away syntactic detail while preserving logical
dependencies. It learns 3x as many tactics as anti-unification baselines,
cuts proof sizes by 26%, and — closing the loop with everything above — the
mined tactics measurably improve LLM-guided proof automation. A PL paper at
heart: good abstractions remain the highest-leverage artifact, even (or
especially) when a model writes the proofs.

## 9. Frontier: formal mathematics and what comes next

Where the strongest capabilities are being forged — and the section that
frames the final discussion about the future of SE and PL research.

### 46. Autoformalization with Large Language Models
*Yuhuai Wu, Albert Q. Jiang, Wenda Li, et al. NeurIPS 2022.*
[Proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/d0c6bc641a56bebee9d985b937307367-Abstract-Conference.html)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** ~187 citations (Semantic Scholar; Google Scholar likely several times higher)

The proof of concept for machine translation from informal mathematics to
formal statements: PaLM/Codex-class models few-shot translate competition
problems into Isabelle/HOL, achieving perfect formalization on 25.3% — and,
in the result that launched a research program, using autoformalized theorems
as training data improves a neural prover on miniF2F from 29.6% to 35.2%.
Autoformalization is the mathematical twin of specification inference
(section 6): in both, the hard problem is faithfully capturing *intent* in a
formal language, at scale.

### 47. Olympiad-Level Formal Mathematical Reasoning with Reinforcement Learning
*Thomas Hubert, Rishi Mehta, Laurent Sartran, et al. (AlphaProof). Nature, 2025.*
[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09833-y)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** too recent for meaningful counts (Nature, Nov 2025)

The capability milestone: AlphaProof, an AlphaZero-style agent that learns
Lean proving via reinforcement learning over millions of auto-formalized
problems, solved three of five non-geometry problems at IMO 2024 — including
the competition's hardest — for a combined silver-medal-standard score with
AlphaGeometry 2. Read as a systems paper: the pipeline *is*
autoformalization (#46) feeding search feeding a verifier, at unprecedented
scale. Discussion: what is the software-verification analogue of "millions of
auto-formalized problems," and who is building it?

### 48. Goedel-Prover: A Frontier Model for Open-Source Automated Theorem Proving
*Yong Lin, Shange Tang, Bohan Lyu, et al. COLM 2025.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.07640)
**Course fit:** ★★★☆☆ · **Impact:** ~43 citations (Semantic Scholar, likely stale)

The open-source counterweight to #47: train statement formalizers to turn
1.64M informal math problems into Lean 4 statements, then iteratively
expert-train whole-proof generators on their own verified successes —
accumulating proofs for 800K+ statements. Successor V2
([arXiv 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03613)) reaches 84.6% on
miniF2F with an 8B model, beating a 671B competitor. The verifier-as-data-
flywheel thesis of #33, industrialized. *See also (fast-moving frontier):*
DeepSeek-Prover-V2 ([arXiv 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.21801)) and
Seed-Prover ([arXiv 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.23726)), which proved
5 of 6 IMO 2025 problems.

### 49. Formal Mathematical Reasoning: A New Frontier in AI
*Kaiyu Yang, Gabriel Poesia, Jingxuan He, et al. ICML 2025 (position paper).*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16075)
**Course fit:** ★★★★☆ · **Impact:** count not retrievable

The right closing reading: a position paper by leaders of the field
(spanning AI and formal methods) arguing that verifier-grounded formal
reasoning is the next frontier for AI — surveying autoformalization, provers,
and verified generation, and laying out open problems and concrete
milestones. Ideal scaffolding for the final synthesis discussion: which of
these milestones would most change software engineering practice, and what
should PL researchers build *now* to be ready? *See also:* "A Survey on Deep
Learning for Theorem Proving" (Li et al.,
[COLM 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09939)) for a comprehensive technical
taxonomy.

## 10. Coda — an open problem: formalizing PL papers themselves

A gap the list above makes visible. Practitioners now routinely sit down
with a frontier LLM, hand it a POPL-style paper, and get a faithful Lean or
Rocq mechanization: the model *invents the definitions* (syntax, semantics,
judgments), states the theorems, and grinds out the proofs interactively
against the checker. **No published paper describes this end-to-end
workflow.** The literature covers the pieces separately: informal-to-formal
proof scaffolding (#40), statement autoformalization over a *fixed*
vocabulary (#46–48), and proof completion *given* an existing formal
development (#39, #42–44). The hard part of mechanizing a research paper —
definitional autoformalization, where the vocabulary itself must be
constructed and definitional fidelity is exactly where formalizations
silently go wrong — is acknowledged as open (#49) but not attacked.
This is arguably the sharpest research opportunity the course surfaces, and
the flagship genre for course projects (see `syllabus.md`).

Two reference points for this discussion, outside the numbered list:

- **Mechanized Metatheory for the Masses: The POPLmark Challenge.**
  *Brian E. Aydemir, Aaron Bohannon, Matthew Fairbairn, et al. (incl.
  Benjamin C. Pierce, Peter Sewell, Stephanie Weirich, Steve Zdancewic).
  TPHOLs 2005.*
  [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/11541868_4) —
  the pre-LLM-era statement of "mechanize PL metatheory" as a community
  benchmark (System F-sub with binding, records, and hairy inductions).
  Twenty years on, the natural seminar question: what does a POPLmark for
  the LLM era look like — formalize *whole papers*, judged on definitional
  fidelity rather than proof completion?
- **miniCTX: Neural Theorem Proving with (Long-)Contexts.**
  *Jiewen Hu, Thomas Zhu, Sean Welleck. ICLR 2025 (Oral).*
  [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03350) — the evaluation setting
  closest to research-paper formalization in the current literature:
  proving theorems that depend on *new, unseen* definitions and context
  from real, growing Lean projects (not a static library), showing
  context-dependence is what separates benchmark proving from real
  formalization work.
